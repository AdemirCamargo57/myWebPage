The Reduced Density Gradient (RDG) is a quantity widely used in quantum chemistry and Non-Covalent Interaction (NCI) analysis. The expression is

\[RDG\left( \mathbf{r} \right) = \frac{1}{2(3\pi^{2})^{1/3}}\frac{\left| \nabla\rho\left( \mathbf{r} \right) \right|}{\rho(\mathbf{r})^{4/3}} \cdot\]

Meaning of each term: \(\rho(\mathbf{r})\) represents the electron density at a point \(\mathbf{r}\) in the space around the molecule. Large \(\rho\) occurs near nuclei, covalent bonds, and lone pairs. Small \(\rho\) occurs in intermolecular regions or far from atoms. The units are usually electrons per volume (\(e/bohr^{3}\)). \(\nabla\rho(\mathbf{r})\) is the gradient of the density

\[\nabla\rho\left( \mathbf{r} \right) = \left( \frac{\partial\rho}{\partial x},\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial y},\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial z} \right).\]

This vector measures how rapidly the electron density changes in space. Its magnitude is given by

\[|\nabla\rho| = \sqrt{\left( \frac{\partial\rho}{\partial x} \right)^{2} + \left( \frac{\partial\rho}{\partial y} \right)^{2} + \left( \frac{\partial\rho}{\partial z} \right)^{2}}\]

A large value means that density changes rapidly. A small value means that the density is nearly constant. The denominator \(\rho^{4/3}\) normalizes the gradient by the local electron density. Without this term, the gradient would be dominated by high-density regions near nuclei. The exponent \(4/3\) comes from the scaling properties of the homogeneous electron gas and the Thomas-Fermi kinetic-energy model. The prefactor

\[\frac{1}{2(3\pi^{2})^{1/3}}\]

is simply a normalization constant. Numerically,

\[\frac{1}{2(3\pi^{2})^{1/3}} \approx 0.1616\]

It is important to note that the RDG is dimensionless.

Physical interpretation: RDG measures how rapidly density changes relative to the local density itself. Low RDG (\(RDG \approx 0\)) occurs when the density is smooth and the gradient is small. It is observed in covalent-bond regions, hydrogen-bond regions, and van der Waals interaction regions. These are exactly the regions highlighted in the NCI (Non-Covalent Interactions) analysis. Large RDG values occur when the density changes abruptly and the density is very low. It is observed far from molecules, near nodal regions, and in regions with little chemical significance.

RDG is the key quantity in the NCI (Non-Covalent Interaction) method developed by Johnson et al. (2010). The computational procedure consists of the following steps: i) compute \(\rho(\mathbf{r})\); ii) compute \(\left| \nabla\rho\left( \mathbf{r} \right) \right|\); iii) compute the Hessian eigenvalues of the density; iv) compute the RDG value at r; v) Plot RDG versus \(sign(\lambda_{2})\rho\), where \(\lambda_{2}\) is the second eigenvalue of the density Hessian. This allows us to identify:

Interaction sign(\(\lambda_{2}\))\(\rho\)
Attractive (H-bond, halogen bond) Negative
van der Waals Near zero
Steric repulsion Positive

For example, consider a hydrogen bond

\[O - H\cdots O.\]

Between the donor and acceptor, the electron density is low, and the gradient is also low. Therefore,

\[RDG = \frac{\text{small}}{\text{small}}\]

produces a characteristic minimum. When plotted as an isosurface (typically RDG = 0.5), the hydrogen bond appears as a colored surface between the interacting atoms.

In programs such as Multiwfn, NCIPLOT, VMD, Chimera, IQA/NCI analysis packages, one commonly plots

\[RDG \times sign\left( \lambda_{2} \right)\rho.\]

The resulting peaks identify hydrogen bonds, π-π stacking, CH···π interactions, van der Waals contacts, and steric effects.

This is why RDG has become one of the most popular descriptors for analyzing weak intermolecular interactions in crystals, supramolecular systems, and biomolecules.

In crystal engineering and supramolecular chemistry, RDG is essentially a tool that converts electron density into a map in which weak interactions become visually detectable and can be classified as attractive, dispersive, or repulsive.

We use the sign of \(\lambda_{2}\) and not \(\lambda_{1}\) or \(\lambda_{3}\ \)because \(\lambda_{2}\) is the eigenvalue that best distinguishes attractive from repulsive interactions in the interaction region. \(\lambda_{1}\) is almost always negative and therefore cannot make this distinction. Let’s examine this question closely. The Hessian of the electron density is given by

\[H(\rho) = \left( \frac{\partial^{2}\rho}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}} \right)\]

and its eigenvalues are conventionally ordered as

\[\lambda_{1} < \lambda_{2} < \lambda_{3}.\]

The corresponding eigenvectors define three mutually perpendicular directions around a point in space. For a bond critical point or interaction region, we typically find

\[\lambda_{1} < 0\]

\[\lambda_{2} < 0\ \text{or}\ > 0\]

\[\lambda_{3} > 0.\]

Each eigenvalue measures the curvature of the electron density along one principal direction. Negative curvature (\(\lambda < 0\)) means that \(\rho\ \)has a local maximum along that direction. Positive curvature (\(\lambda > 0\)) means \(\rho\) has a local minimum along that direction.

In a hydrogen bond,

\[O - H\cdots O\]

At the interaction region, the density accumulates between the donor and the acceptor. The density is concentrated in two perpendicular directions. The Hessian typically looks like

\[\lambda_{1} < 0\]

\[\lambda_{2} < 0\]

\[\lambda_{3} > 0.\]

The two negative eigenvalues indicate the density concentration perpendicular to the interaction axis. The positive eigenvalue corresponds to the interaction direction. Thus

\[\lambda_{2} < 0\]

signals attraction.

In steric repulsion, when two atoms are pushed too close together, the density between them is depleted. The eigenvalues become

\[\lambda_{1} < 0\]

\[\lambda_{2} > 0\]

\[\lambda_{3} > 0.\]

Notice that \(\lambda_{1\ }\)remains negative. Only \(\lambda_{2\ }\)changes sign. This is the key observation. \(\lambda_{1}\) is almost always negative, and it cannot distinguish between the two distinct physical situations. The density is usually concentrated in at least one direction. Therefore

\[\lambda_{1} < 0\]

For hydrogen bonds, van der Waals contacts, steric effects, and covalent bonds. It carries very little information about the type of interaction. If we plotted

\[sign(\lambda_{1})\rho\]

almost every point would appear on the negative side. The attractive and repulsive regions would overlap. The NCI plot would lose its diagnostic power.

The eigenvalue \(\lambda_{3}\) is almost always positive for both attractive and repulsive interactions (\(\lambda_{3} > 0\)). Therefore, \(sign(\lambda_{3})\rho\) would place almost everything on the positive side. Again, we would lose the ability to distinguish attraction from repulsion.

The middle eigenvalue is the one that changes sign according to the nature of the interaction:

Interaction \[\lambda_{1}\] \[\lambda_{2}\] \[\lambda_{3}\]
Covalent bond - - +
Hydrogen bond - - +
van der Waals - ≈0 +
Steric clash - + +

This makes \(\lambda_{2}\) the perfect discriminator.

We can give a geometric interpretation of the eigenvalues. Imagine the density around an interaction region as an ellipsoid. For an attractive interaction, the density is concentrated in two perpendicular directions:

\[\lambda_{1} < 0,\quad\quad\lambda_{2} < 0.\]

The density "bulges" toward the interaction.

For repulsive interaction, the density is concentrated in only one direction:

\[\lambda_{1} < 0,\quad\quad\lambda_{2} > 0.\]

The density is pushed away from the internuclear region. Thus, the sign of \(\lambda_{2}\) tells us whether electron density is accumulating or depleting in the interaction region.

A very common misconception is to associate the eigenvalues \(\lambda_{1}\), \(\lambda_{2}\), and \(\lambda_{3}\) are with the laboratory \(x\), \(y\), and \(z\) axes. They are associated with the principal axes of the density curvature tensor (the Hessian) at each point in space.

We can make an analogy with the inertia tensor. We can remember that for a molecule, the Cartesian axes are arbitrary, and the principal moments of inertia are obtained by diagonalizing the inertia tensor.

The same idea applies here. The Hessian

\[H(\rho) = \begin{pmatrix} \rho_{xx} & \rho_{xy} & \rho_{xz} \\ \rho_{yx} & \rho_{yy} & \rho_{yz} \\ \rho_{zx} & \rho_{zy} & \rho_{zz} \end{pmatrix}\]

is generally not diagonal in the laboratory frame. When we diagonalize it, we obtain \(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3}\) and their corresponding eigenvectors \(\mathbf{e}_{1},\mathbf{e}_{2},\mathbf{e}_{3}.\) These eigenvectors define a local coordinate system that changes from point to point. Therefore, \(\lambda_{1} \neq x,\ \lambda_{2} \neq y,\lambda_{3} \neq z\) in general. Each eigenvector points along a direction where the density curvature is extremal. The corresponding eigenvalue measures the curvature along that direction:

\[\lambda_{i} = \frac{\partial^{2}\rho}{\partial e_{i}^{2}} \cdot\]

So, \(\mathbf{e}_{1}\) points in the direction of the largest negative curvature; \(\mathbf{e}_{2}\) points in the direction of the intermediate curvature, and \(\mathbf{e}_{3}\) points in the direction of the largest positive curvature. For an interaction region between two atoms, such as a hydrogen bond,

\[O - H\cdots O.\]

Near the interaction region, one typically finds \(\lambda_{1} < 0,\ \ \lambda_{2} < 0,\ and\ \lambda_{3} > 0.\) The eigenvectors
\(\mathbf{e}_{3}\) usually point along the interaction axis

\[H\cdots O\]

While \(\mathbf{e}_{1},\mathbf{e}_{2}\) are approximately perpendicular to it. A schematic view is:

e1
↑
|

H--------BCP-------O

Interaction axis ≈ e3, where BCP denotes the bond critical point.

So, we can state that at a bond critical point (or an NCI interaction region), e3 is usually parallel to the interaction line, while e1 and e2 span the plane perpendicular to the interaction. \(\lambda_{2}\) is used because its sign distinguishes attraction from repulsion, not because of its direction.

This distinction is very important in NCI theory, because many papers mention \(\lambda_{2}\) without emphasizing that the physical reason for its use is topological classification of the density, not its orientation in space.

Formula Derivation

A useful derivation starts with the dimensionless density gradient used in DFT. The reduced density gradient is usually written as

\[s\left( \mathbf{r} \right) = \frac{\left| \nabla\rho\left( \mathbf{r} \right) \right|}{2k_{F}\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\rho\left( \mathbf{r} \right)}\]

where \(k_{F}\) is the local Fermi wave vector of a uniform electron gas. For a homogeneous electron gas,

\[k_{F}\left( \mathbf{r} \right) = \left\lbrack 3\pi^{2}\rho\left( \mathbf{r} \right) \right\rbrack^{\frac{1}{3}}.\]

Substituting this into the definition of \(s(\mathbf{r})\), we have

\[s\left( \mathbf{r} \right) = \frac{\left| \nabla\rho\left( \mathbf{r} \right) \right|}{2\left\lbrack 3\pi^{2}\rho\left( \mathbf{r} \right) \right\rbrack^{\frac{1}{3}}\rho\left( \mathbf{r} \right)} \cdot\]

Now separate the density term, we have

\[\left\lbrack 3\pi^{2}\rho(\mathbf{r}) \right\rbrack^{1/3} = (3\pi^{2})^{1/3}\rho(\mathbf{r})^{1/3}\]

Therefore,

\[s(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{|\nabla\rho(\mathbf{r})|}{2(3\pi^{2})^{1/3}\rho(\mathbf{r})^{1/3}\rho(\mathbf{r})}\]

Using \(\rho^{1/3}\rho = \rho^{4/3}\), we obtain

\[s(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{1}{2(3\pi^{2})^{1/3}}\frac{|\nabla\rho(\mathbf{r})|}{\rho(\mathbf{r})^{4/3}}\]

Thus,

\[\boxed{RDG\left( \mathbf{r} \right) = \frac{1}{2(3\pi^{2})^{1/3}}\frac{\left| \nabla\rho\left( \mathbf{r} \right) \right|}{\rho(\mathbf{r})^{4/3}} \cdot}\]

The idea is that RDG compares the rate of spatial variation of electron density with the density expected for a local homogeneous electron gas. Low RDG values in low-density regions are especially useful for identifying weak non-covalent interactions.

The local Fermi wave vector \(k_{F}(\mathbf{r})\) appears in the derivation because RDG was originally designed as a dimensionless measure of density inhomogeneity, comparing the actual electron-density variation to the natural length scale of a homogeneous electron gas (HEG), the reference system underlying most DFT approximations.

Virtually all modern density functional theory begins with the uniform electron gas (UEG) or homogeneous electron gas (HEG). In this model, \(\rho(\mathbf{r}) = \rho_{0}\) is constant everywhere. Because the density is constant,

\[\nabla\rho = 0\]

and, therefore, the system is perfectly homogeneous. The idea is that real molecules differ from this idealized reference, and one would like a quantity that measures how far the local density deviates from homogeneity. One could simply use

\[|\nabla\rho|\]

as a measure of inhomogeneity. However, this quantity is not very meaningful because it depends on the density's absolute magnitude. For example, in region A, we could have

\[\rho = 100\]

\[|\nabla\rho| = 10\]

and in region B, we could have

\[\rho = 0.01\]

\[|\nabla\rho| = 10\]

The same gradient represents very different physical situations. We need a normalized quantity.

What is the natural length scale of an electron gas? In a uniform electron gas, the occupied orbitals fill momentum space up to the Fermi momentum \(k_{F}.\) The electron density and Fermi wave vector are related by

\[\rho = \frac{k_{F}^{3}}{3\pi^{2}} \cdot\]

Solving for \(k_{F}\),

\[k_{F} = (3\pi^{2}\rho)^{1/3}.\]

This quantity defines the characteristic spatial scale of the electron gas. The corresponding length is

\[\mathcal{l}_{F} \sim \frac{1}{k_{F}} \cdot\]

Thus, high density implies large \(k_{F}\) that implies a short length scale, and low density implies small \(k_{F}\) that implies a long length scale. A density gradient has dimensions

\[|\nabla\rho| \sim \frac{\rho}{L} \cdot\]

To make it dimensionless, we divide by a quantity with the same dimensions. Since

\[k_{F} \sim \frac{1}{L}\]

the product \(k_{F}\rho\) has dimensions

\[\frac{\rho}{L} \cdot\]

Therefore,

\[\frac{|\nabla\rho|}{k_{F}\rho}\]

is dimensionless. This is precisely the origin of the reduced gradient. The conventional definition is

\[s\left( \mathbf{r} \right) = \frac{|\nabla\rho|}{2k_{F}\rho} \cdot\]

Historically, the reduced gradient first appeared in the development of Generalized Gradient Approximations (GGAs). The exchange energy of a slowly varying electron gas can be expanded as

\[E_{x} = E_{x}^{LDA} + \text{gradient corrections}.\]

The gradient expansion naturally produces terms involving

\[\left( \frac{|\nabla\rho|}{2k_{F}\rho} \right)^{2}.\]

Thus \(s\) emerged as the natural variable describing departures from uniformity. Later, Johnson's NCI method adopted exactly the same quantity and renamed it the Reduced Density Gradient (RDG).

The RDG is well-suited to non-covalent interactions because, in regions of weak interactions, the density is low and changes slowly. Therefore \(\rho \ll 1\) and \(|\nabla\rho|\) is also small. The reduced gradient becomes small enough to reveal these regions as minima. Hydrogen bonds, π-stacking interactions, and van der Waals contacts all produce characteristic low-RDG regions.

The local Fermi wave vector is not introduced arbitrarily. It is the only intrinsic momentum scale of a homogeneous electron gas. Therefore

\[k_{F}^{- 1}\]

is the only intrinsic length scale available. When we divide the density gradient by \(k_{F}\rho\), we are essentially asking: How rapidly does the density vary compared with the variation expected on the natural length scale of an electron gas of the same density?

This is why the RDG is fundamentally a measure of local density inhomogeneity relative to a uniform electron gas reference. That connection to the homogeneous electron gas is also why RDG, GGAs, the gradient expansion, and the local Fermi wave vector are all intimately connected in density functional theory.

The relation between electron density and the Fermi wave vector is not obvious at first glance because one quantity lives in real space and the other in momentum space.

For a deeper understanding of what the Fermi wave vector is, consider an infinite homogeneous electron gas. The electrons are free particles moving in a box of volume \(V\). The wavefunctions are plane waves:

\[\psi_{\mathbf{k}}\left( \mathbf{r} \right) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{V}}e^{i\mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{r}}.\]

Each state is characterized by a wave vector \(\mathbf{k}\). As the electrons are free particles, they have only kinetic energy, that is,

\[E = \frac{\hslash^{2}}{2m}\nabla^{2}\psi_{\mathbf{k}}\left( \mathbf{r} \right) = \frac{\hslash^{2}k^{2}}{2m} \cdot\]

So,

\[E(k) = \frac{\hslash^{2}k^{2}}{2m}.\]

Using the Pauli principle, at \(T = 0\), electrons occupy the lowest energy states first. Because of spin, each \(\mathbf{k}\) state can contain two electrons: spin up and spin down. As we add electrons, the occupied states fill a sphere in \(k\)-space. The radius of this sphere is called the Fermi wave vector \(k_{F}.\)

Every state inside the sphere is occupied, and every state outside the sphere is empty. The key derivation is to count how many quantum states fit within the Fermi sphere. The volume of a sphere of radius \(k_{F}\) is

\[\frac{4\pi}{3}k_{F}^{3}.\]

In quantum mechanics, each allowed state occupies a volume

\[\frac{(2\pi)^{3}}{V}\]

in \(k\)-space. The factor

\[\frac{(2\pi)^{3}}{V}\]

comes from the quantization of wave vectors in a box. Consider a cubic box of side \(L\). The volume is

\[V = L^{3}.\]

For a free electron, \(\psi(\mathbf{r}) = Ae^{i\mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{r}}.\) To keep the mathematics simple, we impose periodic boundary conditions:

\[\psi(x + L,y,z) = \psi(x,y,z)\]

and, similarly, for \(y\) and \(z\). Applying the boundary condition, we have

\[e^{ik_{x}(x + L)} = e^{ik_{x}x}.\]

Therefore,

\[e^{ik_{x}L} = 1.\]

This occurs only when \(k_{x}L = 2\pi n_{x},\) where \(n_{x} = 0, \pm 1, \pm 2,\ldots\)

Thus

\[\boxed{k_{x} = \frac{2\pi}{L}n_{x}}\]

and similarly

\[k_{y} = \frac{2\pi}{L}n_{y},\quad\quad k_{z} = \frac{2\pi}{L}n_{z}.\]

Adjacent quantum numbers differ by one: \(n_{x} \rightarrow n_{x} + 1.\)

Therefore, the spacing in \(k\)-space is

\[\Delta k_{x} = \frac{2\pi}{L} \cdot\]

Likewise,

\[\Delta k_{y} = \Delta k_{z} = \frac{2\pi}{L} \cdot\]

Each allowed state corresponds to one point in \(k\)-space. The elementary cell surrounding that point has a volume

\[\Delta k_{x}\Delta k_{y}\Delta k_{z}.\]

Therefore,

\[\Delta V_{k} = \left( \frac{2\pi}{L} \right)^{3}.\]

Since \(V = L^{3},\) we obtain

\[\boxed{\Delta V_{k} = \frac{(2\pi)^{3}}{V}}\]

This is the volume occupied by one quantum state in \(k\)-space. This formula is important because if we have some region in \(k\)-space with volume \(V_{k}\), the number of allowed states inside it is approximately

\[N_{\text{states}} = \frac{V_{k}}{\Delta V_{k}} \cdot\]

Substituting, we have

\[N_{\text{states}} = V_{k}\frac{V}{(2\pi)^{3}}.\]

This is the famous density-of-states factor. Let’s apply it to the Fermi sphere. At \(T = 0\), all states inside the sphere

\[k \leq k_{F}\]

are occupied. The sphere volume is

\[V_{k} = \frac{4\pi}{3}k_{F}^{3}.\]

Therefore

\[N = 2\frac{V}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{4\pi}{3}k_{F}^{3}.\]

Factor 2 accounts for spin degeneracy: spin up and spin down. Simplifying,

\[N = \frac{Vk_{F}^{3}}{3\pi^{2}} \cdot\]

Dividing by \(V\),

\[\boxed{\rho = \frac{N}{V} = \frac{k_{F}^{3}}{3\pi^{2}}}\]

which leads directly to

\[\boxed{k_{F} = (3\pi^{2}\rho)^{1/3}.}\]

The factor

\[\frac{(2\pi)^{3}}{V}\]

is simply the volume of the smallest "quantum box" in momentum space. Just as a molecule occupies a finite volume in real space, a quantum state occupies a finite volume in \(k\)-space. Counting how many such little boxes fit inside the Fermi sphere tells us how many electrons can be accommodated, which is why the electron density becomes directly related to the Fermi wave vector.

The equation

\[k_{F} = (3\pi^{2}\rho)^{1/3}\]

tells us that high electron density implies a larger Fermi sphere, and low electron density implies a smaller Fermi sphere. Suppose

\(\rho = 1\) electron/bohr\(^{3}\).

Then

\(k_{F} = (3\pi^{2})^{\frac{1}{3}} \approx 3.09\ \)bohr\(^{- 1}\).

If the density becomes eight times larger, \(\rho = 8,\) then

\[k_{F} = (3\pi^{2} \times 8)^{1/3} = 2(3\pi^{2})^{1/3}.\]

Doubling \(k_{F}\) requires eight times more electrons because the volume of the Fermi sphere scales as \(k_{F}^{3}\).

The DFT uses a local Fermi wave vector because the molecule is not homogeneous. The density changes from point to point: \(\rho = \rho(\mathbf{r}).\) Kohn and Sham introduced the idea: At each point in the molecule, imagine a fictitious homogeneous electron gas having exactly the same density as the real system at that point. Then the local Fermi wave vector is

\[k_{F}(\mathbf{r}) = \left\lbrack 3\pi^{2}\rho(\mathbf{r}) \right\rbrack^{1/3}.\]

Near nuclei, \(\rho\) is large and \(k_{F}\) is large. In intermolecular regions, \(\rho\) is small and \(k_{F}\) is small. This is not the actual momentum of an electron. It is the Fermi wave vector that a homogeneous electron gas would have if its density were equal to the local density of the molecule.

In summary,

\[|\nabla\rho|\]

has dimensions

\[\frac{\text{electron density}}{\text{length}} \cdot\]

To obtain a dimensionless measure, we need a characteristic inverse length. The homogeneous electron gas naturally provides one:

\[k_{F} \sim \frac{1}{L}.\]

Thus

\[s = \frac{|\nabla\rho|}{2k_{F}\rho}\]

Compares the actual rate at which the density changes with the natural spatial scale of an electron gas having the same density. This is why the local Fermi wave vector enters the RDG formula. The electron density tells us how many electrons are present locally. The local Fermi wave vector tells us what momentum distribution a homogeneous electron gas would need to have to accommodate that many electrons. RDG then measures how rapidly the real density varies compared with that of the reference electron gas.

This figure is a Non-Covalent Interaction (NCI) plot, where the Reduced Density Gradient (RDG) is plotted against \(sign(\lambda_{2})\rho\) and is commonly used to analyze hydrogen bonds, van der Waals interactions, and steric repulsions in molecular dynamics simulations.

The two panels correspond to two different times: A) 0.01 ps and B) 3.15 ps. The vertical axis is \(RDG\) and the horizontal axis is \(sign(\lambda_{2})\rho\), where, \(\rho\) = electron density, and \(\lambda_{2}\) = second eigenvalue of the electron density Hessian. The Hessian is

\[H(\rho) = \begin{pmatrix} \frac{\partial^{2}\rho}{\partial x^{2}} & \frac{\partial^{2}\rho}{\partial x\partial y} & \frac{\partial^{2}\rho}{\partial x\partial z} \\ \frac{\partial^{2}\rho}{\partial y\partial x} & \frac{\partial^{2}\rho}{\partial y^{2}} & \frac{\partial^{2}\rho}{\partial y\partial z} \\ \frac{\partial^{2}\rho}{\partial z\partial x} & \frac{\partial^{2}\rho}{\partial z\partial y} & \frac{\partial^{2}\rho}{\partial z^{2}} \end{pmatrix}\]

whose eigenvalues are

\[\lambda_{1} < \lambda_{2} < \lambda_{3}.\]

The sign of \(\lambda_{2}\) tells whether density accumulates or depletes perpendicular to the interaction region. Negative \(\lambda_{2}\) means electron density accumulates between the fragments. These are attractive interactions: Hydrogen bonds, Halogen bonds, Chalcogen bonds, Strong electrostatic attractions. They appear on the left side of the graph (blue region). Positive \(\lambda_{2}\) means density is depleted between fragments. These are repulsive interactions: steric effects and Pauli repulsion. They appear on the right side (in the red region). Near zero (\(\lambda_{2} \approx 0\)) corresponds to very weak interactions: van der Waals contacts and dispersion interactions. These appear near the center (in the green region).

\[sign(\lambda_{2})\rho\]

typically: Blue → attractive; Green → weak vdW; Red → repulsive, which is the standard NCI coloring convention.

The characteristics of "spikes" or "valleys" are important. Whenever an interaction region has low density, the density gradient is also low; therefore, RDG is small, yielding a minimum. The position of the minimum identifies the interaction type.

Panel A (0.01 ps):

The plot is rather smooth. We can see the left minimum around

\[sign(\lambda_{2})\rho \approx - 0.015\]

This indicates an attractive interaction. Likely a hydrogen bond, an ion-dipole interaction, or strong electrostatic contact, depending on the system.

Near

\[sign(\lambda_{2})\rho \approx 0\]

at the center, there is little structure. This suggests a few weak vdW interactions at this instant.

Around

\[sign(\lambda_{2})\rho \approx + 0.015\]

right minimum corresponds to steric repulsion.

Panel B (3.15 ps):

Several narrow spikes appear around

\[sign(\lambda_{2})\rho \approx 0\]

These indicate that the interaction network has changed over time. The appearance of multiple features near zero generally means: new weak intermolecular contacts formed, transient van der Waals contacts appeared, and hydrogen bonds are fluctuating. This is exactly what one expects during a molecular dynamics trajectory.

Comparison of A and B

The most important observation is:

Thus the molecular configuration at 3.15 ps exhibits a richer set of non-covalent contacts.

A possible interpretation would be the RDG versus sign(\(\lambda_{2}\))\(\rho\) plots reveal the evolution of the intermolecular interaction pattern during the simulation. At 0.01 ps the NCI profile is relatively simple, showing distinct attractive and repulsive regions. At 3.15 ps additional spikes emerge near sign(\(\lambda_{2}\))\(\rho\) ≈ 0, indicating the formation of transient weak van der Waals contacts and a more complex network of non-covalent interactions. The persistence of negative sign(\(\lambda_{2}\))\(\rho\) regions demonstrate the presence of attractive interactions, whereas the positive branch corresponds to steric repulsion between electron clouds.
