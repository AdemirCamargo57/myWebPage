Conceptual introduction

When a molecule absorbs an ultraviolet or visible photon, it undergoes a transition from a lower-energy electronic state, typically the ground state \(\Psi_{0}\), to a higher-energy state \(\Psi_{\text{exc}}\). These symbols denote many-electron wavefunctions. Electronic excitation underlies virtually all photochemistry and photophysics: fluorescence, phosphorescence, energy transfer, charge separation in organic solar cells, vision, photosynthesis, photocatalysis, and the colors of dyes.

The central problem is to describe quantitatively and visually how the molecular electron distribution changes upon excitation. The question “Where did the electron come from, and where did it go?” sounds simple, but rarely has a trivial answer.

In the simplest case, excitation corresponds to a single transition between two molecular orbitals (MOs), for example, HOMO \(\rightarrow\) LUMO. HOMO means highest occupied molecular orbital; LUMO means lowest unoccupied molecular orbital. In this idealized case, the hole has the HOMO density, and the promoted electron has the LUMO density. Examining those two orbitals would suffice.

In practice, an excited state can involve many orbital pairs. Configuration interaction with single excitations (CIS), time-dependent Hartree–Fock (TD-HF), and time-dependent density functional theory (TD-DFT) describe single-excitation contributions from an occupied orbital \(i\) to a virtual, or unoccupied, orbital \(a\), with configuration amplitude \(w_{ai}\). In CIS, the state expansion is

Notation and scope. Throughout the real-orbital derivations, \(i,j\) index occupied MOs, \(a,b\) virtual MOs, and \(p,q\) arbitrary MOs. The abbreviations occ and vir mark these sets. The source’s \(w_{ai}\) and the conventional \(w_{i}^{a}\) used in the added derivations denote the same amplitude. The real amplitude \(w_{i}^{a}\) is a coefficient, not a probability; \(\left( w_{i}^{a} \right)^{2}\) is its CIS/TDA weight. The position is \(\mathbf{r} = (x,y,z)\) and \(d\mathbf{r} = dxdydz\); unbounded spatial integrals cover all space. Atomic units set the elementary charge magnitude, electron mass, and reduced Planck constant to one; lengths are in bohr and energies in hartrees. A density integrates to an electron number, not an electrical charge.

Correction on method scope. The following normalized configuration expansion is literal for CIS and useful as a TDA excitation model. Full TD-HF/TD-DFT is a response theory with excitation and de-excitation blocks, not generally a normalized many-electron wavefunction of this form. Spin-adapted spatial amplitudes may carry factors such as \(\sqrt{2}\) in physical transition moments; use the program’s spin and normalization convention consistently.

\[\Psi_{\text{exc}} = \sum_{i \rightarrow a}^{}w_{ai}\,\Psi_{i}^{a}.\]

Here, \(\Psi_{i}^{a}\) is a configuration, represented by a Slater determinant or a spin-adapted combination, obtained by removing an electron from occupied MO \(i\) and placing it in virtual MO \(a\). When many coefficients \(w_{ai}\) are appreciable, inspecting orbitals individually becomes impractical. Hole–electron analysis in Multiwfn addresses this problem. The approach, proposed by Tian Lu and Cheng Zhong, condenses the participating orbital pairs into the real-space hole density \(\rho^{\text{hole}}\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\) and electron density \(\rho^{\text{ele}}\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\). Here r is the three-dimensional position vector. These functions identify where electron density is removed and added, regardless of the number of participating pairs.

These functions, together with the transition density \(T\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\), provide:

The excitation character: local, charge-transfer, or Rydberg.

Quantitative descriptors of charge-transfer distance, spatial extent, and hole–electron overlap.

Electric and magnetic transition dipole moment densities, which connect electronic structure to absorption intensity and optical activity, including circular dichroism.

Real-space representations of the hole and electron

The intuitive picture is that an electron leaves a hole and occupies a new region. For a normalized CIS or Tamm–Dancoff approximation (TDA) excitation, this picture becomes two nonnegative electron-number densities, each integrating to one electron. Their associated electrical charge densities have opposite signs.

First consider CIS or TD-DFT within the TDA, where de-excitation amplitudes are absent. Each configuration \(i \rightarrow a\) has weight \(w_{ai}^{2}\) for real amplitudes. The local (“loc”) terms are weighted sums of the participating orbital densities:

\[\rho_{\text{loc}}^{\text{hole}}\left( \mathbf{r} \right) = \sum_{i \rightarrow a}^{}w_{ai}^{2}\,\varphi_{i}\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\,\varphi_{i}\left( \mathbf{r} \right),\quad\quad\rho_{\text{loc}}^{\text{ele}}\left( \mathbf{r} \right) = \sum_{i \rightarrow a}^{}w_{ai}^{2}\,\varphi_{a}\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\,\varphi_{a}\left( \mathbf{r} \right),\]

Here, \(\varphi_{i}\) is the occupied MO wavefunction, from which density is removed, and \(\varphi_{a}\) is the virtual MO wavefunction, to which density is added. Each pair \(i \rightarrow a\) contributes a fraction \(w_{ai}^{2}\) of orbital \(i\) to the hole and the same fraction of orbital \(a\) to the electron. The notation is

\[\sum_{i \rightarrow a}^{}{} = \sum_{i}^{occ}{\sum_{a}^{vir}.}\]

All orbitals are orthonormal: \(\int\varphi_{p}(\mathbf{r})\varphi_{q}(\mathbf{r})d\mathbf{r} = \delta_{pq}\), where the Kronecker delta \(\delta_{pq}\) equals one if \(p = q\) and zero otherwise. For complex orbitals and amplitudes, the appropriate complex conjugates replace the real products. The cross sums include both ordered pairs \(\left( i,j \right)\) and \(\left( j,i \right)\); do not insert an additional factor of two.

If two orbital pairs share the same destination, for example \(i \rightarrow a\) and \(j \rightarrow a\), their hole contributions can interfere. The cross terms account for this interference:

\[\rho_{\text{cross}}^{\text{hole}}\left( \mathbf{r} \right) = \sum_{\substack{i,j \rightarrow a \\ i \neq j}}^{}w_{ai}\, w_{aj}\,\varphi_{i}\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\,\varphi_{j}\left( \mathbf{r} \right),\quad\quad\rho_{\text{cross}}^{\text{ele}}\left( \mathbf{r} \right) = \sum_{\substack{i \rightarrow a,b \\ a \neq b}}^{}w_{ai}\, w_{bi}\,\varphi_{a}\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\,\varphi_{b}\left( \mathbf{r} \right).\]

For the hole, pairs sharing a virtual orbital \(a\) interfere; for the electron, pairs sharing an occupied orbital \(i\) interfere. Combining local and cross terms gives

\[\boxed{\mspace{6mu}\rho^{\text{hole}}\left( \mathbf{r} \right) = \sum_{i,j\, \rightarrow \, a}^{}w_{ai}\, w_{aj}\,\varphi_{i}\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\,\varphi_{j}\left( \mathbf{r} \right),\quad\quad\rho^{\text{ele}}\left( \mathbf{r} \right) = \sum_{i\, \rightarrow \, a,b}^{}w_{ai}\, w_{bi}\,\varphi_{a}\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\,\varphi_{b}\left( \mathbf{r} \right).\mspace{6mu}}\]

Derivation and a two-configuration example

Let \(\mathbf{W}\) be the rectangular amplitude matrix with entries \(W_{ia} = w_{i}^{a}\). Its rows label occupied orbitals and its columns label virtual orbitals; superscript T denotes transpose. The hole and electron matrices are \(P_{h} = WW^{T}\) and \(P_{e} = W^{T}W\). Expanding their entries gives

\[\left( P_{h})_{ij} = \sum_{a}^{}w_{i}^{a}w_{j}^{a},(P_{e})_{ab} = \sum_{i}^{}w_{i}^{a}w_{i}^{b} \right.\]

The real-space quadratic forms therefore become sums of squares:

\[\rho_{h}(\mathbf{r}) = \sum_{a}^{}\left( \sum_{i}^{}w_{i}^{a}\varphi_{i}(\mathbf{r}) \right)^{2} \geq 0,\]

\[\rho_{e}(\mathbf{r}) = \sum_{i}^{}\left( \sum_{a}^{}w_{i}^{a}\varphi_{a}(\mathbf{r}) \right)^{2} \geq 0.\]

Here subscripts h and e abbreviate hole and ele. Expanding each square gives the diagonal local terms and off-diagonal cross terms above. For two occupied orbitals feeding one virtual orbital, choose

\[W = \left( \begin{array}{r} \sqrt{3}/2 \\ 1/2 \end{array} \right),P_{h} = \begin{pmatrix} 3/4 & \sqrt{3}/4 \\ \sqrt{3}/4 & 1/4 \end{pmatrix},P_{e} = (1).\]

\[\rho_{h} = \frac{3}{4}\varphi_{1}^{2} + \frac{1}{4}\varphi_{2}^{2} + \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\varphi_{1}\varphi_{2},\rho_{e} = \varphi_{a}^{2}.\]

The last hole term is interference. It can be positive or negative locally, but the complete hole density is a square and nonnegative. It integrates to zero because the occupied orbitals are orthogonal. If distinct configurations share neither occupied nor virtual orbitals, their cross contributions vanish.

Generalization to de-excitations in TD-HF and TD-DFT. The response vector contains excitation amplitudes \(w_{ai}\), forming block \(\mathbf{X}\), and de-excitation amplitudes \(w'_{ai}\), forming block \(\mathbf{Y}\), associated with the reverse process \(a \rightarrow i\). The generalized definitions in the referenced Multiwfn manual subtract the de-excitation contributions:

\[\rho^{\text{hole}}\left( \mathbf{r} \right) = \sum_{i,j \rightarrow a}^{}w_{ai}w_{aj}\,\varphi_{i}\varphi_{j}\mspace{6mu} - \mspace{6mu}\sum_{i,j \leftarrow a}^{}w'_{ai}w'_{aj}\,\varphi_{i}\varphi_{j},\]

\[\rho^{\text{ele}}\left( \mathbf{r} \right) = \sum_{i \rightarrow a,b}^{}w_{ai}w_{bi}\,\varphi_{a}\varphi_{b}\mspace{6mu} - \mspace{6mu}\sum_{i \leftarrow a,b}^{}w'_{ai}w'_{bi}\,\varphi_{a}\varphi_{b}.\]

Correction on positivity. For full response theory, set \(X_{ia} = w_{i}^{a}\) and \(Y_{ia} = w_{i}^{'a}\). The manual’s matrices are \(P_{h} = XX^{T} - YY^{T}\) and \(P_{e} = X^{T}X - Y^{T}Y\). A difference of positive semidefinite matrices need not be positive semidefinite. Thus unit normalization alone does not guarantee pointwise nonnegativity. The nonnegative-density overlap formulas require nonnegative distributions; inspect significant negative values instead of silently taking their square roots.

Two essential properties

Normalization. MO orthonormality and amplitude normalization imply that both distributions integrate to one. In CIS/TDA, the sum of squared amplitudes is one; in full response theory, the excitation squared norm minus the de-excitation squared norm is one:

\[\int\rho^{\text{hole}}\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\, d\mathbf{r} = \int\rho^{\text{ele}}\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\, d\mathbf{r} = 1.\]

Derivation. Substitute the hole expansion, exchange the finite sums and integral, and apply orthonormality:

\[\int\rho_{h}d\mathbf{r} = \sum_{ija}^{}w_{i}^{a}w_{j}^{a}\int\varphi_{i}\varphi_{j}d\mathbf{r} = \sum_{ija}^{}w_{i}^{a}w_{j}^{a}\delta_{ij} = \sum_{ia}^{}(w_{i}^{a})^{2} = 1.\]

The electron proof replaces \(\delta_{ij}\) by \(\delta_{ab}\). Equivalently, both integrals equal \(tr(WW^{T}) = tr(W^{T}W)\), where tr is the sum of diagonal matrix entries. With de-excitations the same steps yield

\[\int\rho_{h}d\mathbf{r} = \int\rho_{e}d\mathbf{r} = \sum_{ia}^{}\left\lbrack (w_{i}^{a})^{2} - (w_{i}^{'a})^{2} \right\rbrack = 1.\]

This expresses the transfer of one electron within the adopted excitation model and provides a consistency condition for these distributions.

Absence of orbital phase. The quantities \(\rho^{\text{hole}}\) and \(\rho^{\text{ele}}\) are densities, not wavefunctions. In CIS/TDA they are nonnegative; positive and negative electrical charges are assigned to the hole and electron separately. They do not retain a wavefunction phase. Natural transition orbitals (NTOs) provide an orbital representation when phase patterns are needed. The full-response positivity limitation is discussed above.

NTO example and matrix meaning. A singular value decomposition is \(W = U\Sigma V^{T}\), where U and V have orthonormal singular-vector columns and the diagonal matrix \(\Sigma\) contains nonnegative singular values \(s_{k}\). Index k labels NTO pairs. The occupied and virtual combinations are \(h_{k} = \sum_{i}^{}U_{ik}\varphi_{i}\) and \(e_{k} = \sum_{a}^{}V_{ak}\varphi_{a}\). Orthogonality then gives

\[T(\mathbf{r};\mathbf{r}') = \sum_{k}^{}s_{k}h_{k}(\mathbf{r})e_{k}(\mathbf{r}'),\rho_{h} = \sum_{k}^{}s_{k}^{2}h_{k}^{2},\rho_{e} = \sum_{k}^{}s_{k}^{2}e_{k}^{2}.\]

For the preceding two-configuration example there is one nonzero singular value, \(s_{1} = 1\): \(h_{1} = (\sqrt{3}\varphi_{1} + \varphi_{2})/2\) and \(e_{1} = \varphi_{a}\). NTOs reveal the coherent orbital combination; densities retain its squared magnitude rather than an arbitrary global phase.

2.2. Hole–electron overlap functions

Two overlap functions describe where, and to what extent, the hole and electron coexist:

\[S_{m}\left( \mathbf{r} \right) = min\left\lbrack \rho^{\text{hole}}\left( \mathbf{r} \right),\,\rho^{\text{ele}}\left( \mathbf{r} \right) \right\rbrack,\quad\quad S_{r}\left( \mathbf{r} \right) = \sqrt{\rho^{\text{hole}}\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\,\rho^{\text{ele}}\left( \mathbf{r} \right)}.\]

\(S_{m}\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\) takes the smaller density at each point and is appreciable only where both distributions are appreciable. \(S_{r}\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\) is their geometric mean and generally gives smoother visualizations and a convenient mathematical interpretation. Integration gives the indices \(S_{m}\) and \(S_{r}\), with \(S_{r} \geq S_{m}\):

\[S_{m}\,\text{index} = \int min\left\lbrack \rho^{\text{hole}},\rho^{\text{ele}} \right\rbrack\, d\mathbf{r},\quad\quad S_{r}\,\text{index} = \int\sqrt{\rho^{\text{hole}}\,\rho^{\text{ele}}}\, d\mathbf{r}.\]

Bounds. For nonnegative numbers u and v, assume \(u \leq v\). Then \(u^{2} \leq uv\), so \(min(u,v) = u \leq \sqrt{uv}\). Integration proves \(S_{m} \leq S_{r}\). The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality applied to the square roots of normalized densities gives

\[0 \leq S_{m} \leq S_{r} = \int\sqrt{\rho_{h}}\sqrt{\rho_{e}}d\mathbf{r} \leq \sqrt{\int\rho_{h}d\mathbf{r}}\sqrt{\int\rho_{e}d\mathbf{r}} = 1.\]

Example. Identical normalized densities give \(S_{m} = S_{r} = 1\); disjoint supports give zero. If electron and hole are uniform on equal-volume regions of volume V whose intersection has volume \(V_{o}\), both indices equal \(\frac{V_{o}}{V}\). V and \(V_{o}\) are spatial volumes.

A value near one indicates substantial overlap, common in local excitations. A value near zero indicates little overlap, which can result from charge transfer or a diffuse Rydberg electron. Overlap alone does not classify an excitation.

2.3. Charge density difference

The net electron-number-density change associated with excitation is commonly called the charge density difference (CDD):

\[\rho^{\text{CDD}}\left( \mathbf{r} \right) = \rho^{\text{ele}}\left( \mathbf{r} \right) - \rho^{\text{hole}}\left( \mathbf{r} \right).\]

Where \(\rho^{\text{CDD}} > 0\), electron density increases; where \(\rho^{\text{CDD}} < 0\), it decreases. Each distribution integrates to one, so the CDD integrates to zero, expressing conservation of electron number and total charge.

\[\int\rho_{CDD}d\mathbf{r} = \int\rho_{e}d\mathbf{r} - \int\rho_{h}d\mathbf{r} = 1 - 1 = 0.\]

Example. Suppose a donor fragment has hole population 0.80 and electron population 0.20. Its CDD is −0.60 electron. A complementary acceptor has populations 0.20 and 0.80, giving +0.60 electron; the sum is zero. Electrical charge changes have the opposite signs.

Practical note for Gaussian users. In the manual’s convention, this CDD corresponds to the unrelaxed excited-state density, \(\rho^{\text{exc}} = \rho^{\text{fund}} - \rho^{\text{hole}} + \rho^{\text{ele}}\), constructed from MOs and configuration amplitudes. The subscripts exc and fund denote excited and ground states. It differs from a difference involving the relaxed density exported using the Z-vector response treatment. The manual specifies density=rhoci when generating an excited-state .wfn or .wfx file for the corresponding unrelaxed comparison.

2.4. Transition density

For real many-electron wavefunctions, the spin-integrated one-electron transition density matrix between ground and excited states is

\[T\left( \mathbf{r};\mathbf{r}' \right) = N\int\Psi_{0}\left( \mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{N} \right)\,\Psi_{\text{exc}}\left( \mathbf{x}',\mathbf{x}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{N} \right)\, d\sigma\, d\mathbf{x}_{2}\cdots d\mathbf{x}_{N},\]

Here, \(\mathbf{x}\) combines spatial and spin coordinates: x = (r, σ) and x′ = (r′, σ). N is the electron number; \(x_{2}\) through \(x_{N}\) are the remaining electron coordinates. Integration over σ means spin summation, and integration over each electron coordinate includes spatial integration and spin summation. Applying the Slater–Condon rules gives, in the adopted orbital-amplitude convention,

\[T\left( \mathbf{r};\mathbf{r}' \right) = \sum_{i \rightarrow a}^{}w_{ai}\,\varphi_{i}\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\,\varphi_{a}\left( \mathbf{r}' \right).\]

Derivation. The one-electron transition operator can connect the reference determinant only to a determinant differing by one occupied-to-virtual substitution. The integrations over the other \(N - 1\) electrons produce overlap factors; orthonormal spectator orbitals contribute one. The surviving active-electron factor is \(\varphi_{i}(\mathbf{r})\varphi_{a}(\mathbf{r}')\). Linearity of the excited-state expansion then produces the displayed sum. This explains both orbital indices of the kernel and why its diagonal is a product rather than a squared orbital density.

Taking the diagonal, \(\mathbf{r}' = \mathbf{r}\), gives the transition density, a scalar function of position:

\[\boxed{\mspace{6mu} T\left( \mathbf{r} \right) = \sum_{i \rightarrow a}^{}w_{ai}\,\varphi_{i}\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\,\varphi_{a}\left( \mathbf{r} \right).\mspace{6mu}}\]

For a unit-amplitude HOMO\(\rightarrow\)LUMO transition, this becomes

\[T\left( \mathbf{r} \right) = \varphi_{\text{HOMO}}\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\,\varphi_{\text{LUMO}}\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\]

the product of the initial and final orbital wavefunctions.

Zero integral. Orthonormality of occupied and virtual MOs gives

\[\int T\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\, d\mathbf{r} = 0.\]

\[\int T(\mathbf{r})d\mathbf{r} = \sum_{ia}^{}w_{i}^{a}\int\varphi_{i}\varphi_{a}d\mathbf{r} = \sum_{ia}^{}w_{i}^{a}\delta_{ia} = 0.\]

The last equality holds because occupied and virtual orbital sets are disjoint. More generally, integration of the physical transition density gives \(N\langle\Psi_{0}|\Psi_{exc}\rangle = 0\) for orthogonal states.

A nonzero real transition density therefore has positive and negative regions whose integrals cancel.

Hole–electron coupling. A large magnitude of \(\left| T\left( \mathbf{r} \right) \right|\) indicates a substantial local transition amplitude. However, \(T\left( \mathbf{r} \right) \approx 0\) does not generally imply negligible overlap: orbital-pair contributions can cancel. The relationship between \(T\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\) and \(S_{r}\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\) is an equality in magnitude for one unit-amplitude real orbital transition, but not for a general mixture. For states with different total spin, for example \(S_{0} \rightarrow T_{1}\), the physical kernel \(T\left( \mathbf{r};\mathbf{r}' \right)\) vanishes for spin-pure states and a spin-independent density operator. Multiwfn can display a spatial-only \(T\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\) for singlet–triplet analysis; this is a diagnostic field, not a nonzero physical electric transition density. Correction: small transition density does not always imply small overlap.

Cancellation example. At a point where \(\varphi_{1} = \varphi_{2} = \varphi_{a} = u \neq 0\), take two amplitudes \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\) and \(- 1/\sqrt{2}\) for transitions from orbitals 1 and 2 into distinct virtual orbitals a and b with \(\varphi_{b} = u\). Then \(T = 0\) locally although \(\rho_{h} = \rho_{e} = u^{2}\). This uses pairs sharing neither orbital, so the hole/electron cross terms vanish. Here u denotes the common orbital value, not a normalized state.

2.5. Electric transition dipole moment density

The electric transition dipole moment

\[\mathbf{D} = \langle\Psi_{0}\left| \hat{\mathbf{\mu}} \right|\Psi_{\text{exc}}\rangle\]

governs electric-dipole absorption intensity. Here μ is the electric dipole operator, and the brackets denote a matrix element. Its electronic part is the negative sum of electron position vectors in atomic units; the fixed-nuclei contribution vanishes between orthogonal states. The component densities are minus the coordinate times the transition density, because the electron charge is \(- 1\) in atomic units:

\[T_{x}\left( \mathbf{r} \right) = - x\, T\left( \mathbf{r} \right),\quad\quad T_{y}\left( \mathbf{r} \right) = - y\, T\left( \mathbf{r} \right),\quad\quad T_{z}\left( \mathbf{r} \right) = - z\, T\left( \mathbf{r} \right).\]

Integration over all space recovers the Cartesian transition-moment components:

\[D_{x} = \int T_{x}\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\, d\mathbf{r},\quad\quad D_{y} = \int T_{y}\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\, d\mathbf{r},\quad\quad D_{z} = \int T_{z}\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\, d\mathbf{r}.\]

Example and origin check. In one dimension use normalized harmonic-oscillator functions \(\varphi_{0} = \pi^{- 1/4}e^{- x^{2}/2}\) and \(\varphi_{1} = \sqrt{2}x\varphi_{0}\), with oscillator length set to one. Shared normalized transverse factors extend the example to three dimensions. Then

\[T(x) = \sqrt{2}x\varphi_{0}^{2},\int T(x)dx = 0,D_{x} = - \sqrt{2}\int x^{2}\varphi_{0}^{2}dx = - 1/\sqrt{2}.\]

The first integral vanishes by odd parity; the second uses the Gaussian variance \(\int x^{2}\varphi_{0}^{2}dx = 1/2\). Under an origin shift \(\mathbf{r} \mapsto \mathbf{r} - \mathbf{a}\), where a is a constant vector, the dipole changes by \(\mathbf{a}\int Td\mathbf{r} = 0\). Thus its integral is origin independent, although the local dipole-density map changes.

Plots of \(T_{x}\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\), \(T_{y}\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\), and \(T_{z}\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\) identify regions contributing constructively or destructively to the electric transition dipole and hence absorption intensity. The vector D here is a transition moment, distinct from the charge-transfer distance D in Section 4.1.

2.6. Magnetic transition dipole moment density

The orbital magnetic dipole operator is proportional to orbital angular momentum, \(\hat{\mathbf{L}} = - i\,\left( \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{\nabla} \right)\). The italic i is the imaginary unit, i² = −1; ∇ is the spatial gradient. In Cartesian components,

\[\hat{\mathbf{L}} = - i\left\lbrack \mathbf{i}\left( y\frac{\partial}{\partial z} - z\frac{\partial}{\partial y} \right) + \mathbf{j}\left( z\frac{\partial}{\partial x} - x\frac{\partial}{\partial z} \right) + \mathbf{k}\left( x\frac{\partial}{\partial y} - y\frac{\partial}{\partial x} \right) \right\rbrack.\]

The bold i, j, and k are Cartesian unit vectors, distinct from orbital indices and the imaginary unit. The \(x\) component of the real magnetic-transition quantity reported in the manual’s convention, suppressing imaginary and overall sign factors and using \(\leftarrow\) for de-excitations, is

\[M_{x} = \sum_{i \rightarrow a}^{}w_{ai}\left\langle \varphi_{i}\left| \, y\frac{\partial}{\partial z} - z\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\, \right|\varphi_{a} \right\rangle\mspace{6mu} - \mspace{6mu}\sum_{j \leftarrow b}^{}w'_{bj}\left\langle \varphi_{j}\left| \, y\frac{\partial}{\partial z} - z\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\, \right|\varphi_{b} \right\rangle,\]

\(M_{y}\) and \(M_{z}\) follow by cyclic coordinate permutation. Convention note. To convert the numerical magnitude of \(M\) reported by Multiwfn to the commonly used convention

\[\frac{1}{2i}\langle\psi_{i}|\mathbf{r} \times \nabla|\psi_{j}\rangle,\]

Notation correction: the factor is written as an explicit fraction with denominator 2i, matching the manual.

divide by two. The symbols \(\psi_{i}\) and \(\psi_{j}\) denote the states entering that convention. Correction: this factor converts the magnitude; it does not restore the imaginary factor, sign, or bra–ket ordering. These must be retained when calculating rotatory strength.

The magnetic transition dipole moment density \(m_{i}\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\) is defined by

\[M_{i} = \int m_{i}\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\, d\mathbf{r.}\]

Here i is a Cartesian label, i ∈ {x, y, z}, not an occupied-orbital index. The \(x\) component is

\[m_{x}\left( \mathbf{r} \right) = \sum_{i \rightarrow a}^{}w_{ai}\,\varphi_{i}\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\left\lbrack y\frac{\partial\varphi_{a}}{\partial z} - z\frac{\partial\varphi_{a}}{\partial y} \right\rbrack\mspace{6mu} - \mspace{6mu}\sum_{j \leftarrow b}^{}w'_{bj}\,\varphi_{j}\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\left\lbrack y\frac{\partial\varphi_{b}}{\partial z} - z\frac{\partial\varphi_{b}}{\partial y} \right\rbrack.\]

Derivation of the de-excitation sign. Define \(A_{x} = y\partial_{z} - z\partial_{y}\), with cyclic definitions of \(A_{y},A_{z}\). For orbitals decaying at infinity, integration by parts gives \(\int\varphi_{i}A_{x}\varphi_{a}d\mathbf{r} = - \int\varphi_{a}A_{x}\varphi_{i}d\mathbf{r}\): the boundary term vanishes and \(\partial_{z}y = \partial_{y}z = 0\). The antisymmetry produces the excitation-minus-de-excitation combination. The symmetric position operator instead uses the appropriate excitation-plus-de-excitation response combination in physical electric moments.

Example. Let \(g(\mathbf{r}) = \pi^{- 3/4}e^{- r^{2}/2}\), \(p_{x} = \sqrt{2}xg\), and \(p_{y} = \sqrt{2}yg\). These are normalized Cartesian oscillator orbitals. Since \(A_{z} = x\partial_{y} - y\partial_{x}\), differentiation gives \(A_{z}p_{y} = p_{x}\). Therefore \(M_{z} = \int p_{x}A_{z}p_{y}d\mathbf{r} = \int p_{x}^{2}d\mathbf{r} = 1\), and \(m_{z} = p_{x}^{2}\). In the manual’s half-angular-momentum convention the magnitude is 1/2; its complex factor must still be restored.

Plots of \(m_{x}\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\), \(m_{y}\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\), and \(m_{z}\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\) identify regions contributing to angular motion of electronic charge associated with the transition, supporting interpretation of chiral optical activity. These component fields are not the full vector current density.

3. Chemical interpretation of hole–electron distributions and transition density

The hole \(\rho^{\text{hole}}\) identifies electron-density removal and characterizes the donating region. It often has HOMO-like character, such as a donor \(\pi\) system, a lone pair, or an electron-rich group. The symbol π denotes pi orbital symmetry relative to the bonding framework.

The electron \(\rho^{\text{ele}}\) identifies electron-density addition, often with LUMO-like character: an accepting \(\pi^{*}\) system, an electron-withdrawing group, or diffuse Rydberg orbitals. An asterisk denotes antibonding character.

The CDD, \(\rho^{\text{CDD}} = \rho^{\text{ele}} - \rho^{\text{hole}}\), gives the balance between electron-density loss, where the hole dominates, and gain, where the electron dominates. It maps redistribution between states, not an electron trajectory.

The transition density \(T\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\) provides complementary information about electronic coherence rather than population change. Regions of large magnitude of \(\left| T\left( \mathbf{r} \right) \right|\) contribute to the oscillatory density of a coherent state superposition. Weighting the transition density by position and integrating gives the electric transition dipole. The distinction is

\(\rho^{\text{hole}}\) and \(\rho^{\text{ele}}\) describe where electron density is removed and added, emphasizing populations.

\(T\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\) describes the spatial transition amplitude that couples states through a one-electron operator, emphasizing coherence.

Why “oscillation” is appropriate. Let \(c_{0},c_{n}\) be complex superposition amplitudes, τ time, \(E_{0},E_{n}\) state energies, and an asterisk complex conjugation. A coherent superposition evolves as \(\Psi(\tau) = c_{0}e^{- iE_{0}\tau}\Psi_{0} + c_{n}e^{- iE_{n}\tau}\Psi_{n}\). Its number density is

\[\rho(\mathbf{r},\tau) = |c_{0}|^{2}\rho_{0} + |c_{n}|^{2}\rho_{n} + 2Re\left\lbrack c_{0}^{*}c_{n}e^{- i(E_{n} - E_{0})\tau}T_{0n}(\mathbf{r}) \right\rbrack.\]

Re denotes real part, and \(T_{0n}\) is the physical transition density between states 0 and n. Expanding the squared superposition gives two population terms and two conjugate cross terms, whose sum is twice their real part. A stationary excited state alone does not have this oscillating interference density.

3.1. Decomposition into orbitals, atoms, and fragments

Decomposing the distributions into MO, atomic, and fragment contributions makes the analysis chemically interpretable.

MO contributions. The contributions of occupied MO \(i\) to the hole and virtual MO \(a\) to the electron are

\[\Theta_{i}^{\text{hole}} = \sum_{a}^{}\left\lbrack \left( w_{ai} \right)^{2} - \left( w'_{ai} \right)^{2} \right\rbrack,\quad\quad\Theta_{a}^{\text{ele}} = \sum_{i}^{}\left\lbrack \left( w_{ai} \right)^{2} - \left( w'_{ai} \right)^{2} \right\rbrack,\]

with normalizations \(\sum_{i}^{}\Theta_{i}^{\text{hole}} = 1\) and \(\sum_{a}^{}\Theta_{a}^{\text{ele}} = 1\). Θ is a dimensionless integrated contribution; its subscript identifies an orbital, atom, or fragment, and its superscript identifies hole or electron. These values reveal dominant orbitals.

Atomic and fragment contributions. Real-space partitions such as Hirshfeld, Becke, and iterative Hirshfeld (Hirshfeld-I), or a Mulliken-like partition, assign fractions to atom A. For the Hirshfeld hole contribution,

\[\Theta_{A}^{\text{hole}} = \int w_{A}^{\text{Hirsh}}\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\,\rho^{\text{hole}}\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\, d\mathbf{r},\]

\(w_{A}^{\text{Hirsh}}\) is the dimensionless Hirshfeld weight of atom A. The electron expression replaces the hole density by the electron density. Summing over a fragment gives

\[\Theta_{\text{frag}}^{\text{hole}} = \sum_{A \in \text{frag}}^{}\Theta_{A}^{\text{hole}},\quad\quad\Theta_{\text{frag}}^{\text{ele}} = \sum_{A \in \text{frag}}^{}\Theta_{A}^{\text{ele}}.\]

Here frag is the specified set of atoms and A ∈ frag means membership in that set. The electron-population change is the integrated atomic CDD:

\[\text{CDD}_{A} = \Theta_{A}^{\text{ele}} - \Theta_{A}^{\text{hole}}.\]

A negative value indicates net donation; a positive value indicates acceptance. Contributions can be plotted as heat maps. Correction: separate hole and electron populations are marginal contributions, not a complete atom-to-atom or fragment-to-fragment transfer matrix. Pairwise transfer requires an additional transition-matrix analysis.

Choice of partition. Mulliken-like partitioning is fast and often useful, but diffuse functions can cause unreliable or negative atomic populations. For anions, Rydberg states, or substantial negative contributions, a real-space partition such as Hirshfeld is generally more robust, though more expensive. This addresses partition artifacts, not the separate positivity limitation of full-response densities.

Partition example and normalization. For Hirshfeld weights \(w_{A}(\mathbf{r}) = \rho_{A}^{0}(\mathbf{r})/\sum_{B}^{}\rho_{B}^{0}(\mathbf{r})\), \(\rho_{A}^{0}\) is a reference free-atom density and B runs over all atoms. Thus \(\sum_{A}^{}w_{A} = 1\) wherever the denominator is nonzero. Consequently \(\sum_{A}^{}\Theta_{A}^{h} = \int(\sum_{A}^{}w_{A})\rho_{h}d\mathbf{r} = 1\), and likewise for the electron. Adding the two donor atoms with hole contributions 0.35 and 0.45 gives a donor hole population of 0.80. Contributions depend on the partition and should be compared using one consistent scheme.

4. Excitation types and spectroscopic connections

Numerical indices summarize the distributions and help classify excitation character. The principal descriptors follow.

4.1. Centroids and charge-transfer distance

A centroid is the density-weighted mean position. For the electron’s \(X\) coordinate,

\[X_{\text{ele}} = \frac{\int x\,\rho^{\text{ele}}\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\, d\mathbf{r}}{\int\rho^{\text{ele}}\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\, d\mathbf{r}}\mathbf{=}\frac{\int x\,\rho^{\text{ele}}\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\, d\mathbf{r}}{1}\mathbf{=}\int x\,\rho^{\text{ele}}\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\, d\mathbf{r},\]

with analogous expressions for \(Y\), \(Z\), and the hole. Uppercase X, Y, Z are centroid coordinates; lowercase x, y, z are position variables. The componentwise distances are

\[D_{x} = |X_{ele} - X_{hole}|,D_{y} = |Y_{ele} - Y_{hole}|,D_{z} = |Z_{ele} - Z_{hole}|.\]

Correction: the source wrote signed component differences. Absolute values have been restored here to match the manual’s componentwise distances; the total Euclidean distance is unaffected.

Here \(D_{x}\), \(D_{y}\), and \(D_{z}\) are nonnegative centroid separations, distinct from electric transition moment components. The total charge-transfer distance is

\[\boxed{\mspace{6mu} D_{\text{index}} = \sqrt{D_{x}^{2} + D_{y}^{2} + D_{z}^{2}}.\mspace{6mu}}\]

A large D indicates separated centroids and commonly signals charge transfer (CT). For the adopted unrelaxed density and fixed nuclear geometry, the permanent-dipole change is \(\Delta\mu_{x} = - \left( X_{\text{ele}} - X_{\text{hole}} \right)\), with analogous y and z expressions. This change is signed, unlike the componentwise distances.

Derivation and example. At fixed nuclei, \(\Delta\mathbf{\mu} = - \int\mathbf{r}\rho_{CDD}d\mathbf{r} = - (\mathbf{R}_{e} - \mathbf{R}_{h})\), where \(\mathbf{R}_{e},\mathbf{R}_{h}\) are centroid vectors. If \(\mathbf{R}_{h} = (0,0,0)\) and \(\mathbf{R}_{e} = (4,0,0)\) bohr, the distance is 4 bohr and the permanent-dipole change is \(\left( - 4,0,0 \right)\) atomic units. This is unrelated to the electric transition moment between the states.

4.2. Spatial extent and effective separation

The root-mean-square deviation (RMSD), or standard deviation about the centroid, measures spatial extent. For the hole along \(X\),

\[\sigma_{\text{hole},x} = \sqrt{\int\left( x - X_{\text{hole}} \right)^{2}\,\rho^{\text{hole}}\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\, d\mathbf{r}}.\]

The magnitudes \(\left| \mathbf{\sigma}_{\text{hole}} \right|\) and \(\left| \mathbf{\sigma}_{\text{ele}} \right|\) are the overall extent indices \(\sigma_{\text{hole}}\) and \(\sigma_{\text{ele}}\). Each bold σ is the vector of Cartesian standard deviations; its magnitude is the Euclidean norm. The \(H\) index gives their mean overall extent:

\[H_{\text{index}} = \frac{\left| \mathbf{\sigma}_{\text{ele}} \right| + \left| \mathbf{\sigma}_{\text{hole}} \right|}{2},\]

\(H_{\text{CT}}\) is the mean extent projected onto the charge-transfer direction. The t index compares centroid distance with that extent:

\[\boxed{\mspace{6mu} t_{\text{index}} = D_{\text{index}} - H_{\text{CT}}.\mspace{6mu}}\]

Completing the definitions. For \(\lambda \in \{ x,y,z\}\), define \(H_{\lambda} = (\sigma_{e,\lambda} + \sigma_{h,\lambda})/2\) and \(\mathbf{H} = (H_{x},H_{y},H_{z})\). For nonzero centroid displacement, \(\mathbf{u}_{CT} = (\mathbf{R}_{e} - \mathbf{R}_{h})/D\) is its unit vector and the manual defines \(H_{CT} = |\mathbf{H} \cdot \mathbf{u}_{CT}|\). The component and overall extent differences are

\[\Delta\sigma_{\lambda} = \sigma_{e,\lambda} - \sigma_{h,\lambda},\Delta\sigma = |\mathbf{\sigma}_{e}| - |\mathbf{\sigma}_{h}|.\]

For the RMSD formula, expansion of the square gives \[\sigma_{h,x}^{2} = \langle x^{2}\rangle_{h} - 2X_{h}\langle x\rangle_{h} + X_{h}^{2}\langle 1\rangle_{h} = \langle x^{2}\rangle_{h} - X_{h}^{2}\]. Angle brackets here mean a density-weighted spatial average. This proves the equivalence of the central-moment and second-moment forms.

Example. Let two isotropic normalized Gaussian densities have centroid separation 4 bohr along x and Cartesian standard deviations 1 bohr. Then \(H_{CT} = 1\) bohr and \(t = 3\) bohr, while each overall extent and H equal \(\sqrt{3}\) bohr. The geometric-mean overlap is \(S_{r} = e^{- D^{2}/(8s^{2})} = e^{- 2} \approx 0.1353\), with Gaussian width \(s = 1\) bohr. Completing the square in the product of the two Gaussian densities yields this overlap. Positive t does not imply zero overlap.

Correction and limitation. At D = 0 the unit vector, hence t, is undefined without an additional convention. The manual’s projection is a heuristic based on Cartesian standard deviations. A rigorous directional variance uses a covariance matrix \(C_{h}\) with entries \((C_{h})_{\lambda\kappa} = \langle(r_{\lambda} - R_{h,\lambda})(r_{\kappa} - R_{h,\kappa})\rangle_{h}\) and width \(\sqrt{\mathbf{u}^{T}C_{h}\mathbf{u}}\). This is distinct from the manual’s index and should not silently replace it.

The condition \(t < 0\) means that projected extent exceeds centroid separation. A clearly positive \(t\) indicates appreciable separation along the CT direction. The \(t\) index supplements \(D\); it is a heuristic, not a proof of disjoint support or a universal CT classifier. At coincident centroids the CT direction is undefined.

4.3. Delocalization indices

The hole delocalization index (HDI) and electron delocalization index (EDI) quantify density concentration:

\[HDI = 100\sqrt{\int\rho_{hole}(\mathbf{r})^{2}d\mathbf{r}},EDI = 100\sqrt{\int\rho_{ele}(\mathbf{r})^{2}d\mathbf{r}}.\]

Correction: the source omitted square roots in HDI and EDI. The displayed definitions restore the square roots from the manual.

Example. For a uniform normalized density \(\rho = 1/V\) within volume V, \(\int\rho^{2}d\mathbf{r} = V/V^{2} = 1/V\), so the manual’s index is \(\frac{100}{\sqrt{V}}\). Doubling V reduces it by \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\). The prefactor 100 is a scale factor, not a percentage; with lengths in bohr the index carries inverse-volume square-root units.

Smaller HDI or EDI indicates greater spatial delocalization. They are useful for distributions concentrated in several distinct regions, where large \(\sigma\) may reflect separated localized peaks rather than density spread uniformly throughout space.

4.4. Empirical classification

The following empirical guide combines the descriptors. Correction: the table restores the manual’s unspecified CT entries; small overlap and positive t are common for strongly separated unidirectional CT, but are not universal requirements. Δσ denotes the difference in overall electron and hole extents defined above.

Excitation type D Sᵣ t Δσ
Local (LE) Small Medium–large < 0 Small
Unidirectional CT Large Unspecified Unspecified Unspecified
Centrosymmetric CT Small Unspecified < 0* Large
Rydberg Small Small < 0* Large

* The manual’s heuristic entry presumes a defined CT direction; at exactly coincident centroids t is undefined. “Unspecified” corresponds to the manual’s question marks.

Local excitation (LE)

Hole and electron occupy similar regions, with substantial overlap, large \(S_{r}\), and little centroid separation. Compact chromophores undergoing \(\pi \rightarrow \pi^{*}\) excitation often show this pattern.

Charge-transfer excitation (CT)

Electron density redistributes between regions. CT can be unidirectional, with large D, or multidirectional. Centrosymmetric CT is a special case: signed displacements cancel, producing small \(D\) despite substantial redistribution. Other indices and spatial plots are therefore essential.

Rydberg excitation

The electron occupies very diffuse MOs and typically overlaps weakly with the compact hole. Large spatial extent need not produce a large centroid displacement.

4.5. Exciton binding energy

The negative electron and positive hole attract electrostatically. The negative of their attractive Coulomb interaction energy is the positive Coulomb binding descriptor

\[E_{C} = \iint\frac{\rho^{\text{hole}}\left( \mathbf{r}_{1} \right)\,\rho^{\text{ele}}\left( \mathbf{r}_{2} \right)}{\left| \mathbf{r}_{1} - \mathbf{r}_{2} \right|}\, d\mathbf{r}_{1}\, d\mathbf{r}_{2}\quad\text{(atomic units)}.\]

Here \(\mathbf{r}_{1},\mathbf{r}_{2}\) are independent integration positions and \(\left| \mathbf{r}_{1}-\mathbf{r}_{2} \right|\) is their separation. Multiplying charge densities gives a negative interaction; defining \(E_{C} = - U_{C}\) makes the displayed descriptor positive. For compact distributions separated by R much larger than their widths, replace the denominator by R. Normalization then gives \(E_{C} \approx (1/R)(\int\rho_{h})(\int\rho_{e}) = 1/R\). At R = 10 bohr this is about 0.10 hartree, whereas at 20 bohr it is about 0.05 hartree.

Large values indicate stronger attraction, often associated with compact pairs; small values indicate weaker attraction, as can occur for separated distributions. This is relevant to photovoltaic materials where charge separation is desired. Correction: this descriptor is not generally the full exciton binding energy defined by the fundamental gap minus the optical gap. It omits exchange, relaxation, and correlation contributions discussed in the manual.

5. Transition dipole moments in spectroscopy and photophysics

5.1. Electric transition dipole moment and absorption intensity

The electric transition dipole moment \(\mathbf{D} = \langle\Psi_{0}\left| \hat{\mathbf{\mu}} \right|\Psi_{\text{exc}}\rangle\) connects electronic structure to absorption. Fermi’s golden rule gives a polarization-resolved probability proportional to the squared projection onto the light polarization. After orientational averaging the dependence is proportional to \(\left| \mathbf{D} \right|^{2}\). The dimensionless oscillator strength is

\[f = \frac{2}{3}\,\Delta E\,\left| \mathbf{D} \right|^{2}\quad\text{(atomic units)},\]

Derivation and example. For unit light-polarization vector \(\mathbf{\epsilon}\), electric-dipole coupling is proportional to \(\mathbf{\epsilon} \cdot \mathbf{D}\). Isotropic averaging gives \(\langle|\mathbf{\epsilon} \cdot \mathbf{D}|^{2}\rangle = |\mathbf{D}|^{2}/3\) because the three Cartesian directions are equivalent. In the atomic-unit oscillator-strength definition this yields \(f = 2\Delta E|\mathbf{D}|^{2}/3\). If \(\Delta E = 0.20\) hartree and \(\mathbf{D} = (1,0,0)\) atomic units, f = 0.1333. Reducing every dipole component by ten reduces f by one hundred.

where \(\Delta E\) is the excitation energy in hartrees and D is the electric transition moment in atomic units. A transition with \(\mathbf{D} \approx 0\) is electric-dipole forbidden or nearly dark; large \(\left| \mathbf{D} \right|\) generally gives appreciable intensity at comparable excitation energy.

Because \(\mathbf{D} = \int\mathbf{T}\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\, d\mathbf{r}\), with \(T_{x} = - x\, T\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\) and analogous components, the dipole density shows how intensity is assembled. Regions with the same sign of \(T_{x}\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\) add constructively; opposite signs cancel. Thus substantial population redistribution can coexist with a nearly vanishing transition moment. Symmetry-forbidden transitions are an extreme example.

5.2. Magnetic transition dipole moment and chiral optical activity

The magnetic transition quantity \(\mathbf{M}\) describes the angular-motion contribution. Magnetic-dipole absorption is usually much weaker than electric-dipole absorption in ordinary molecular spectra. Its interference with the electric moment is central to electronic circular dichroism (ECD) and circularly polarized luminescence (CPL).

The central quantity is the rotatory strength, the imaginary part of a scalar product of electric and magnetic transition matrix elements:

\[R = Im\left( \langle\Psi_{0}\left| \hat{\mathbf{\mu}} \right|\Psi_{n}\rangle \cdot \langle\Psi_{n}\left| \hat{\mathbf{m}} \right|\Psi_{0}\rangle \right)\mspace{6mu} \propto \mspace{6mu}\mathbf{D} \cdot \mathbf{M}.\]

Here \(\Psi_{n}\) is excited state n, m is the magnetic dipole operator, Im means imaginary part, and the dot is a vector scalar product. The sign of \(R\) determines the ECD sign under a specified convention; its magnitude contributes to band strength. In an achiral isotropic system the net strength is zero. For a nondegenerate symmetry-resolved transition this may follow from \(R = 0\), \(\mathbf{D} = 0\), \(\mathbf{M} = 0\), or \(\mathbf{D}\bot\mathbf{M}\). In chiral systems the relative orientation of \(\mathbf{D}\) and \(\mathbf{M}\) matters. Plotting \(\mathbf{T}\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\) and \(\mathbf{m}\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\) helps locate spatial contributions. Correction: the proportionality assumes consistent phase, sign, and bra–ket conventions, and degenerate bands require the appropriate summed strength.

Example with an explicit phase convention. Choose \(\langle 0|\mathbf{\mu}|n\rangle = (d,0,0)\) and \(\langle n|\mathbf{m}|0\rangle = (im,0,0)\), where d and m are real numbers in compatible units. Then \(R = Im(idm) = dm\). Reversing the magnetic sign reverses R; making the moments perpendicular gives zero. This illustrates why an unsigned ratio of magnitudes cannot determine optical activity.

5.3. Relevance to photoinduced processes

These quantities also inform interpretation of excited-state dynamics:

Photoinduced charge transfer. The indices \(D\) and \(t\) and the Coulomb binding descriptor characterize tendencies relevant to organic photovoltaics and donor–acceptor dyads. Correction: they do not independently predict charge-separation yields; energetics, couplings, nuclear motion, and environment also matter.

Radiative decay. The oscillator strength \(f\), through \(\mathbf{D}\), relates to spontaneous emission. At fixed emission frequency, larger \(\mathbf{D}\) gives a faster radiative rate. Brightness also depends on competing nonradiative decay and excited-state populations.

Intersystem crossing and phosphorescence. The character of \(T\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\) and the singlet/triplet hole–electron distributions can help interpret spin–orbit coupling and nonradiative pathways. Quantitative rates and spin-forbidden intensities require coupling matrix elements and state mixing.

CPL emitter design. The relative magnitudes and orientation of \(\mathbf{M}\) and \(\mathbf{D}\), with consistent electromagnetic units and phases, determine luminescence dissymmetry. Correction: their magnitude ratio alone is insufficient.

Photophysical examples. In vacuum the electric-dipole spontaneous-emission rate scales as \(A \propto \omega^{3}|\mathbf{\mu}_{n0}|^{2}\), where A is the rate and ω is emission angular frequency; doubling the moment at fixed ω quadruples A. Fluorescence yield is \(\Phi_{f} = k_{r}/(k_{r} + k_{nr})\) for a simple two-channel model, where \(k_{r}\) and \(k_{nr}\) are radiative and nonradiative rates. Even a large transition moment can accompany weak fluorescence if nonradiative decay dominates.

For compatible electric/magnetic units and a fixed CPL sign convention, the electric–magnetic dipole approximation has the form \(g_{lum} = 4Im(\mathbf{\mu} \cdot \mathbf{m}^{*})/(|\mathbf{\mu}|^{2} + |\mathbf{m}|^{2})\), with the sign depending on the declared emission and polarization convention. The physical complex moments must be used. The absolute bound \(|g_{lum}| \leq 4ab/(a^{2} + b^{2}) \leq 2\), where \(a = |\mathbf{\mu}|\) and \(b = |\mathbf{m}|\), follows from Cauchy–Schwarz and \(\left( a - b)^{2} \geq 0 \right.\ \). At b/a = 0.01, the maximal magnitude is approximately 0.04, but orthogonal moments give zero.

6. Interpreting visualizations

Multiwfn evaluates the functions on a spatial grid and exports .cube files for isosurfaces, for example in Visual Molecular Dynamics (VMD), or planar contour maps. Practical guidelines follow.

Hole and electron isosurfaces. Two colors usually distinguish them, for example blue for hole and green for electron. Nonnegative densities have single-sign isosurfaces. Overlapping clouds \(\Rightarrow\) suggest local excitation; different molecular regions \(\Rightarrow\) suggest CT, with hole\(\rightarrow\)electron indicating redistribution. A diffuse electron halo \(\Rightarrow\) suggests Rydberg character.

CDD isosurfaces. Two colors indicate signs: a region with \(\rho^{\text{CDD}} < 0\), for example blue, shows loss; a region with \(\rho^{\text{CDD}} > 0\), for example green, shows gain. This reveals which groups lose and gain electron population.

Atomic and fragment heat maps. Show separate populations with atoms/fragments as rows and hole/electron as columns. A true fragment-to-fragment map requires a pairwise transfer measure and explicit row/column conventions.

Transition-density isosurfaces \(T\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\). A nonzero real \(T\) generally has positive and negative lobes, possibly many rather than exactly two. Large magnitude of \(|T|\) indicates substantial transition amplitude. Alternating signs in conjugated chains are common in \(\pi \rightarrow \pi^{*}\) transitions. Overall sign depends on state phase convention.

Electric and magnetic dipole densities \(\mathbf{T}\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\) and \(\mathbf{m}\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\). Components \(x\), \(y\), and \(z\) reveal constructive or destructive contributions to \(\mathbf{D}\) and \(\mathbf{M}\). An imbalance of signed volume integrals \(\Rightarrow\) gives a larger component; symmetric cancellation \(\Rightarrow\) gives a small one. Correction: visible surface area alone does not measure an integral, and a large magnetic moment alone does not imply intense ordinary absorption.

Isovalue selection. Compare the same quantity across states or molecules at the same isovalue, for example 0.002 atomic units for electron-number density. High values hide diffuse tails important for Rydberg/CT character; very low values can reveal numerical noise or obscure the main features. Different fields have different units, so do not apply one numerical threshold indiscriminately.

Numerical example. A diffuse lobe with peak density 0.0015 in the chosen units disappears at an isovalue of 0.002. It may appear at 0.001 even though the electronic state has not changed. Check grid extent and convergence, and verify that numerical integrals approach the expected normalization; a picture alone cannot validate a computed density.

7. Simple examples illustrating physical meaning

Example 1. A pure HOMO\(\mathbf{\rightarrow}\)LUMO transition

If \(w_{ai} = 1\) for the HOMO–LUMO pair and all other amplitudes vanish, then \(\rho^{\text{hole}} = \left| \varphi_{\text{HOMO}} \right|^{2}\), \(\rho^{\text{ele}} = \left| \varphi_{\text{LUMO}} \right|^{2}\), and \(T\left( \mathbf{r} \right) = \varphi_{\text{HOMO}}\varphi_{\text{LUMO}}\). The HOMO\(\rightarrow\)LUMO pair gives the result. Hole and electron are exactly the HOMO and LUMO densities. This checks the formalism, subject to the stated spin and normalization convention.

Example 2. Ethylene and a local \(\mathbf{\pi}\mathbf{\rightarrow}\mathbf{\pi}^{\mathbf{*}}\) excitation

The hole has bonding \(\pi\) character and the electron antibonding \(\pi^{*}\) character. Both occupy the carbon–carbon bonding region, typically giving large \(S_{r}\), small \(D\), and \(t < 0\), a local excitation. The transition density lies in this region, and the allowed \(\pi \rightarrow \pi^{*}\) band has appreciable intensity. Pi density lies above and below the molecular plane, not on the internuclear axis.

Example 3. A donor–bridge–acceptor system

In a push–pull molecule such as dimethylamino-substituted nitrobenzene, excitation may remove density from the amino donor and add it to the nitro acceptor. Strongly separated CT gives large \(D\), \(t > 0\), and small \(S_{r}\). The CDD shows loss at one end and gain at the other. Large \(D\) implies large magnitude of \(\Delta\mu\) in the unrelaxed model and can contribute to strong solvatochromism; the solvent response also matters.

Example 4. Centrosymmetric CT with cancelling displacements

In an inversion-symmetric molecule with two accepting arms, density can move from the center to both sides. Signed displacements cancel: \(D_{\text{index}} \approx 0\), despite substantial redistribution. The \(D\) index alone misleads. The source lists \(t < 0\), large \(\Delta\sigma\), and small \(S_{r}\) as indicators. Correction: t has no unique direction at coincident centroids, and the manual leaves centrosymmetric CT overlap unspecified. Inspect the distributions and extents rather than treating these entries as strict conditions.

Example 5. A symmetry-forbidden transition

In benzene, \(^{1}A_{1g} \rightarrow^{1}B_{2u}\) can involve appreciable redistribution while its electric transition moment vanishes: \(\mathbf{D} \approx 0\), \(f \approx 0\), in the ideal electronic electric-dipole approximation. The state labels are symmetry representations; superscript 1 means singlet, and g/u mean even/odd inversion parity. Plotting \(\mathbf{T}\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\) alone does not establish dipole cancellation: its unweighted integral vanishes for all orthogonal transitions. Correction: inspect coordinate-weighted dipole densities, whose integrals vanish here by symmetry. Vibronic coupling can give the observed band weak intensity.

8. Summary of essential points

The single-excitation description contains pairs \(i \rightarrow a\) with amplitudes \(w_{ai}\); squared real CIS/TDA amplitudes are weights. Hole–electron analysis combines them into real-space distributions.

Hole \(\rho^{\text{hole}}\) describes density removal; electron \(\rho^{\text{ele}}\) describes addition. Both integrate to one under the stated normalization and lack orbital phase.

The densities contain local terms with weights \(w_{ai}^{2}\) and interference terms from pairs sharing an orbital. The referenced full TD-DFT convention subtracts de-excitations, subject to the normalization and positivity qualifications in Section 2.1.

CDD \(= \rho^{\text{ele}} - \rho^{\text{hole}}\) maps net density redistribution and integrates to zero.

Transition density \(T\left( \mathbf{r} \right) = \sum_{i \rightarrow a}^{}w_{ai}\varphi_{i}\varphi_{a}\) describes local transition amplitude. Its integral is zero, but operator-weighted integrals give transition moments.

Electric dipole density \(T_{i}\left( \mathbf{r} \right) = - x_{i}T\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\) integrates to \(\mathbf{D}\), entering the oscillator strength through \(f \propto \Delta E\,\left| \mathbf{D} \right|^{2}\). The component label i means x, y, or z.

Magnetic dipole density \(m_{i}\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\) integrates to \(\mathbf{M}\) in the adopted convention. Combined with \(\mathbf{D}\) through rotatory strength, schematically \(R \propto \mathbf{D} \cdot \mathbf{M}\) with consistent phases and units, it contributes to ECD and CPL.

Indices \(S_{r}\), \(S_{m}\) measure overlap; \(D\) centroid distance; \(t\) projected separation; \(\sigma\)/\(H\) extent; HDI/EDI delocalization; and \(E_{C}\) Coulomb attraction. Together with plots, they help distinguish local, CT, and Rydberg excitations.

Decomposition into MOs, atoms, and fragments using Hirshfeld or Mulliken-like partitions supports chemical interpretation and heat maps.

Use single-sign plots for nonnegative hole/electron densities, and two-sign plots for CDD, \(T\), and dipole component densities. Keep isovalues consistent for the same quantity.

9. Conceptual review questions

Why represent hole and electron by densities rather than wavefunctions? What phase information is lost, and how do NTOs provide an orbital interpretation?

Explain why \(\int\rho^{\text{hole}}\, d\mathbf{r} = \int\rho^{\text{ele}}\, d\mathbf{r} = 1\) under the adopted normalization. What physical consistency does this express?

What distinguishes local and cross terms in \(\rho^{\text{hole}}\) and \(\rho^{\text{ele}}\)? When do cross terms vanish or become negligible?

Distinguish transition density \(T\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\) from CDD \(\rho^{\text{CDD}}\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\). Associate “where charge oscillates in a coherent superposition” and “where density is removed and added” with the correct quantity and justify.

Why does \(\int T\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\, d\mathbf{r} = 0\), while \(\int T_{x}\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\, d\mathbf{r} = D_{x}\) can be nonzero? Consider the factor \(- x\).

How can substantial CDD coexist with nearly zero oscillator strength? What would \(\mathbf{T}\left( \mathbf{r} \right)\), especially its coordinate-weighted components, reveal?

If \(D\) is nearly zero in a centrosymmetric molecule despite substantial redistribution, which other descriptors and plots would you use, and why?

A hole lies on an amino group and an electron on a nitro group at opposite molecular ends. What excitation character is suggested? What trends do you expect for \(D\), \(t\), \(S_{r}\), and the permanent-dipole change?

Why is the magnetic transition moment usually minor in ordinary absorption but essential to circular dichroism? What combines \(\mathbf{D}\) and \(\mathbf{M}\), and what determines an ECD band’s sign?

Explain the Coulomb exciton binding descriptor. Why can a smaller value favor photovoltaic charge separation, and why does it not fully predict performance?

When are Mulliken-like hole/electron populations unreliable, and why is Hirshfeld preferable then?

Why compare the same density at the same isovalue for different states? What can an inappropriate threshold conceal?
